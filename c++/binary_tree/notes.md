The origins of the Indian caste system (known as *Varna* and *Jati*) are a subject of significant historical and genetic debate. It did not appear overnight but evolved over thousands of years from a flexible social structure into a rigid, hereditary hierarchy.

### **The Origins: Vedic Period ( BCE)**

The earliest literary mention of the division of society is found in the **Rig Veda**, specifically in the *Purusha Sukta* hymn.

* **The Concept:** It describes the creation of four *Varnas* (classes) from the body of a primordial being, *Purusha*:
* **Brahmins (Priests/Teachers):** From the mouth.
* **Kshatriyas (Warriors/Rulers):** From the arms.
* **Vaishyas (Traders/Farmers):** From the thighs.
* **Shudras (Laborers/Servants):** From the feet.


* **The Reality:** During the Early Vedic period, these categories were likely **occupational rather than hereditary**. There is evidence that individuals could change their status based on their skills or character.

---

### **The Shift to Rigidity ( BCE –  CE)**

The transition from a fluid class system to a rigid, oppressive caste system coincided with the writing of **Dharmashastras** (legal and ethical texts).

* **The Manusmriti (Laws of Manu):** Written roughly between 200 BCE and 200 CE, this text is often cited as the foundational document for caste-based discrimination. It codified the idea that birth determines one's status and introduced strict rules against **inter-caste marriage** (*endogamy*).
* **Genetic Evidence:** Modern genetic studies (notably by the Harvard Medical School and the Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology) suggest that widespread intermixing between Indian populations stopped abruptly around **1,900 to 2,000 years ago**. This marks the point when social barriers became biological ones.

---

### **The First Documented Caste Oppression**

Identifying the "first" single act of oppression is difficult because it developed through systemic exclusion, but we can pinpoint when the concept of **"Untouchability"** first appeared in the record.

#### **1. Exclusion of the "Avarna" ( CE)**

By the Gupta Empire period, a fifth group emerged outside the four Varnas: the **Chandala** (often considered the ancestors of modern Dalits).

* The **Manusmriti** explicitly details their oppression, stating they must live outside the village, use discarded bowls, and wear the clothes of the dead.

#### **2. First External Documentation (5th Century CE)**

The Chinese Buddhist monk **Faxian**, who traveled through India around 400 CE, provided some of the first "eyewitness" accounts of caste-based social exclusion. He wrote:

> "The Chandalas are named 'evil men' and dwell apart from others. When they enter a gate to a city or market, they strike a piece of wood to make themselves known, so that men may know and avoid them, and not come into contact with them."

This is one of the earliest recorded descriptions of **enforced social distancing** and public humiliation based on caste identity.

---

### **Why did it become so entrenched?**

The system was maintained through a combination of **religious justification** (the doctrine of Karma and Rebirth), **economic control** (restricting certain groups to "unclean" or low-paying labor), and **political backing** by various ruling dynasties who used the hierarchy to maintain social order.

The *Manusmriti* (also known as the *Laws of Manu*), composed between **200 BCE and 200 CE**, is the most influential—and controversial—legal text in the Hindu tradition regarding social stratification. It transformed the functional *Varna* classes into a rigid, hereditary hierarchy.

The treatment of the **Chandala** in this text represents the first codified system of "untouchability" and extreme social exclusion in Indian history.

---

### **The Origin of the Chandala**

According to the *Manusmriti*, the Chandala were not part of the original four *Varnas* (Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras). Instead, they were classified as **Pratiloma** (literally "against the hair" or "against the grain").

* **The "Illicit" Birth:** The text defines a Chandala as the offspring of a **Brahmin woman** and a **Shudra man**.
* **The Lowest Status:** Because this union was considered the most "polluting" violation of social order, the resulting child was placed at the very bottom of the social hierarchy, lower even than the Shudras.

---

### **Codified Oppression: The Laws of Exclusion**

The *Manusmriti* (specifically Chapter 10) outlines a series of "duties" and restrictions for Chandalas that were designed to ensure their permanent segregation and dehumanization.

| Category | Mandated Restriction in *Manusmriti* |
| --- | --- |
| **Residence** | They must live **outside the village** or town boundaries. |
| **Possessions** | They are forbidden from owning cattle or donkeys; their wealth must consist only of **dogs and donkeys**. |
| **Clothing** | They must wear the **garments of the dead** and ornaments made of black iron. |
| **Food** | Their food must be served in **broken clay dishes**, and they must eat only what is given to them by others. |
| **Movement** | They are prohibited from walking in villages or cities at night. During the day, they must carry a **distinguishing mark** so others can avoid them. |
| **Labor** | Their primary duty was the **disposal of unclaimed corpses** and serving as executioners for criminals. |

---

### **The Concept of "Pollution"**

The *Manusmriti* introduced a metaphysical dimension to this oppression. It wasn't just about social class; it was about **ritual purity**.

* The text argued that the mere sight or shadow of a Chandala could "pollute" a member of a higher caste.
* This necessitated the "striking of wood" (as noted by the traveler Faxian later) to warn others of their approach, effectively making them "invisible" or "untouchable" in polite society.

---

### **Historical Significance**

While it is debated how strictly these laws were enforced across all of India at all times, the *Manusmriti* provided the **legal and theological framework** for the caste-based discrimination that persisted for nearly two millennia.

* **The "Outcastes":** This group eventually became known as *Panchamas* (the fifth group) or *Avarnas* (those without Varna).
* **Modern Context:** In the 20th century, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, the architect of the Indian Constitution and a Dalit leader, famously burned the *Manusmriti* in 1927 as a protest against the "sacred" justification for untouchability.

Determining exact population percentages for the period between **200 BCE and 200 CE** is challenging because formal censuses did not exist. However, historians and sociologists use archaeological data, literary texts (like the *Manusmriti* and *Arthashastra*), and genetic studies to estimate social structures.

### **The "Avarna" Population: 200 BCE – 200 CE**

During this period, the term **"Avarna"** (those outside the four *Varnas*) primarily referred to tribal groups, forest dwellers, and the emerging "untouchable" communities like the Chandalas.

* **Estimated Percentage:** Most historians suggest that the Avarna and Shudra (laboring class) populations combined likely made up **70% to 80%** of the total population.
* **The "Outcaste" Slice:** The specific group of "untouchables" (Avarnas) was likely a smaller fraction—perhaps **10% to 15%**—consisting of conquered indigenous tribes and those pushed to the margins of the expanding agrarian economy.
* **The Transition:** At this time, many people were still transitioning from tribal lifestyles into the caste hierarchy. The "Avarna" status was often a "holding pen" for groups that the Brahmanical system had not yet assimilated into the Shudra category.

### **What Happened After 200 AD?**

The period following 200 CE (marking the rise of the **Gupta Empire** and the "Classical Age") saw the caste system harden from a social theory into a rigid, inescapable reality.

#### **1. The "Freezing" of the Hierarchy**

Genetic research indicates that **endogamy** (marrying only within one's caste) became strictly enforced around **100–400 CE**. Before this, there was significant genetic mixing; after 200 CE, the "caste walls" went up, and the genetic lineages of different groups began to diverge.

#### **2. Proliferation of Jatis (Sub-castes)**

As the economy became more complex, the four broad *Varnas* shattered into thousands of **Jatis**.

* Guilds (professional groups like weavers, blacksmiths, or potters) turned into hereditary castes.
* If your father was a tanner, you were now legally and socially required to be a tanner.

#### **3. Expansion of Untouchability**

The "Avarna" category grew significantly. As Hindu kingdoms expanded into forest lands, they didn't bring the tribal inhabitants into the fold as equals. Instead, they were categorized as "untouchable" to perform "polluting" tasks (cleaning, leatherwork, handling the dead) that the four Varnas refused to do.

#### **4. The Land Grant System**

Starting around 300–500 CE, kings began granting entire villages to Brahmins (the *Agrahara* system).

* This localized power.
* The local peasants and "Avarna" laborers became tied to the land, effectively creating a **feudal-caste system** where social status and economic survival were inseparable.

The period between **400 CE** (the height of the Gupta Empire) and the **12th Century** (the establishment of the Delhi Sultanate) is often called the "Early Medieval" period of India. During this time, the caste system transitioned from a set of religious guidelines into a rigid, inescapable social and economic engine.

Here is how the status of the **Avarna** and the broader caste structure evolved during those 800 years:

---

### **1. The Rise of the "Feudal-Caste" System**

After the decline of the centralized Gupta Empire (c. 550 CE), India fractured into many regional kingdoms. To maintain control, kings began the **Agrahara system**—granting land and entire villages to Brahmins and temples.

* **Economic Serfdom:** The "Avarna" and lower-caste Shudras who lived on these lands were effectively "gifted" along with the soil. They became tied to the land, losing the freedom to move or change their occupation.
* **The Proliferation of Jatis:** Because these communities were isolated in specific villages, thousands of localized sub-castes (**Jatis**) formed. Your identity wasn't just "Shudra"; it was "the weaver from this specific village."

### **2. The Expansion of "Untouchability"**

During this period, the list of "polluting" professions expanded significantly. Groups that were once simply "low status" were pushed into the "Untouchable" (Avarna) category.

* **New "Pollutants":** Occupations like leather-working, cleaning, and fishing were increasingly viewed as spiritually infectious.
* **The "Fifth Varna" (Panchamas):** By the 7th and 8th centuries, the "Untouchables" were a distinct, massive segment of the population, completely excluded from the four-fold Varna system.
* **Adi Shankara and Philosophy:** While high-level Hindu philosophy (like Advaita Vedanta) argued that all souls were one, in practice, social laws became harsher. The *Smritis* written in this era reinforced that Shudras and Avarnas should not hear the Vedas.

### **3. Genetic and Social "Hardening"**

By roughly **600–800 CE**, the "genetic walls" were fully built.

* **Endogamy (Marrying Within):** Regional councils (Caste Panchayats) became the enforcers of marriage laws. Breaking these rules could lead to being "outcasted," effectively a social death sentence.
* **The Role of Temples:** Temples became the center of social life, but they also became the site of the most visible exclusion. Avarnas were strictly forbidden from entering temple grounds, a practice that would persist for over a millennium.

---

### **4. The Rise of the Bhakti Movement (7th–12th Century)**

As the caste system reached its peak of rigidity, a massive internal "rebellion" began in South India: the **Bhakti Movement**.

* **The Alvars and Nayanars:** These saint-poets in Tamil Nadu (6th–9th century) came from all castes, including the "Untouchable" Panar community.
* **The Message:** They preached that **devotion (Bhakti)** to God was superior to ritual and caste status.
* **The Paradox:** While the Bhakti movement offered spiritual equality, it rarely changed the **social or economic** reality of the Avarna. You could be a saint in the eyes of God, but you still had to live outside the village in the eyes of the law.

---

### **5. The Arrival of Islam (11th–12th Century)**

When Islamic rule began to take hold (starting with the raids of Mahmud of Ghazni and the later conquests of Muhammad Ghori), it introduced a radical alternative to the caste hierarchy.

* **The Appeal of Equality:** Islam’s doctrine of "All believers are equal before God" was a massive shock to the caste-stratified society.
* **Mass Conversions:** Many Avarna and lower-caste groups converted to Islam. In many cases, entire **Jatis** (occupational groups) converted together to escape the stigma of "untouchability."
* **The Result:** This forced the Hindu elite to further tighten caste rules to prevent "seepage" into Islam, leading to even stricter social controls within Hindu communities.

---

The period between **1000 CE and 1500 CE** saw a "siege mentality" develop within the Hindu social elite. As Islamic rule expanded, the Brahminical leadership feared that the egalitarian appeal of Islam—where a King and a beggar could pray in the same mosque—would lead to a total collapse of the traditional social order.

To prevent this "seepage," they didn't loosen the rules to make Hinduism more attractive; instead, they **hardened the boundaries** to make leaving the fold nearly impossible or socially devastating.

---

### **1. The Doctrine of "Kalivarjya" (Forbidden in the Kali Age)**

During this era, a legal concept called *Kalivarjya* was heavily promoted. It argued that because society was in the "Age of Vice" (Kali Yuga), many ancient flexibilities were now dangerous and therefore forbidden.

* **Prohibition of Sea Travel (*Kala Pani*):** Crossing the ocean was declared a "sin" that resulted in the immediate loss of caste. This was designed to prevent Hindus from interacting with "Mlechhas" (foreigners/non-believers) and bringing back "corrupting" ideas.
* **Abolition of Re-conversion:** Once a person converted to Islam (or was even "polluted" by eating with a Muslim), the rules were rewritten to make it almost impossible for them to return to the Hindu fold. This created a "one-way door" that paradoxically made those remaining within the system more terrified of accidental pollution.

### **2. The Weaponization of "Purity and Pollution"**

To distinguish Hindu identity from the ruling Islamic class, the elite intensified ritual purity laws.

* **Commensality (Eating Together):** Rules regarding who could cook for whom and who could sit together became hyper-refined. If a lower-caste person’s shadow fell on a Brahmin’s food, it was discarded.
* **Water Sources:** Access to village wells became a primary site of oppression. Avarna people were strictly barred from "savarna" (caste-Hindu) wells to ensure no physical "mixing" occurred between the two worlds.

### **3. Women as the "Gatekeepers" of Caste**

The elite realized that the caste system could only survive if bloodlines remained "pure." This led to a drastic decline in the status of women across all castes, but especially among the upper tiers.

* **Child Marriage:** To prevent young women from potentially choosing partners from other castes (or the new ruling class), child marriage became the norm. Girls were married off before they could develop independent social connections.
* **Strict Endogamy:** The *Jati* councils (Caste Panchayats) became much more aggressive. If a family allowed a daughter to marry "out," the entire extended family was ostracized, losing their livelihood and social standing.

### **4. The "Taxation of Identity" (The Jizya Factor)**

While the Islamic rulers imposed the **Jizya** tax on non-Muslims, the Hindu elite often used the existing caste structure to manage this burden.

* **Hierarchy in Submission:** Often, the upper-caste local leaders (*Zunmindaars* or *Deshmukhs*) acted as the tax collectors for the Sultanate. They used their traditional caste authority to squeeze the lower-caste and Avarna laborers even harder to meet the Sultan's demands while protecting their own status.
* **Social Stigma as a Barrier:** To prevent lower castes from converting to escape the Jizya or to gain social status, the Hindu elite intensified the social stigma against converts, labeling them as "outcasts" who had betrayed their ancestors, thus cutting them off from their village support networks.

---

### **The Result: A Fractured Society**

By the time the Mughal Empire reached its peak, Hindu society had essentially "curled into a ball." The caste system was no longer just a religious hierarchy; it was a **defensive shell**.

While this shell successfully preserved Hindu culture under centuries of foreign rule, it did so by institutionalizing the extreme oppression of the **Avarna** people, making their "untouchability" more rigid than it had ever been in the Vedic or Gupta periods.

The period from **1500 to the mid-1700s** (predominantly the **Mughal Era**) represents a fascinating paradox for the caste system. While the ruling elite was Islamic and technically practiced egalitarianism, the social reality for the **Avarna** (Dalit/Outcaste) population remained one of extreme stagnation and localized oppression.

---

### **1. The Mughal "Hands-Off" Policy (1526–1707)**

The Mughals were pragmatic rulers. To maintain a massive empire, they relied on the existing Hindu social structure to collect taxes and maintain order.

* **Collaboration with Rajputs and Brahmins:** Emperors like Akbar integrated Hindu elites into the imperial bureaucracy (*Mansabdari* system). This meant the Mughal state effectively **validated the caste hierarchy** by empowering traditional land-owning castes to govern the peasantry.
* **Caste as a Tax Category:** The Mughals didn't try to abolish "untouchability." In land records, certain groups were categorized as *Kamin* (low/servile) or *Paikas* (laborers). Their "Avarna" status made them a permanent, cheap labor force that the Mughal economy depended on for agriculture and leather production (essential for the military).

### **2. The "Village Republic" and the Jajmani System**

During this time, the **Jajmani system** became the economic backbone of rural India. It was a system of hereditary social obligations.

* **Fixed Roles:** An Avarna family was born into a "service" relationship with a Landlord (*Jajman*). They performed "polluting" tasks (cleaning, carcass removal) in exchange for a small portion of the harvest.
* **No Exit:** Because there was no free labor market, an Avarna person could not quit or move to another village. The Mughal state recognized the landlord's right to "his" laborers, effectively making the caste system a form of **hereditary serfdom**.

---

### **3. The Radical Challenge: The Bhakti and Sikh Movements**

While the law was rigid, the 16th and 17th centuries saw the greatest spiritual revolt against caste in Indian history.

* **Sant Kabir and Ravidas (15th–16th Century):** **Guru Ravidas**, an Avarna saint from the leather-working community, famously spoke of "Begumpura" (a city without sorrow), where there was no caste or taxes. His followers directly challenged the idea that Avarnas were "polluted."
* **The Rise of Sikhism:** Founded by **Guru Nanak**, Sikhism explicitly rejected the Varna system. The introduction of **Langar** (the community kitchen where everyone eats together) was a direct strike at the heart of "commensality" laws (rules against eating with lower castes).
* **The Khalsa (1699):** When Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa, the first five initiates (*Panj Pyare*) came from various castes, including the lower tiers. This provided a militant and social escape for many, though "caste-consciousness" often persisted even after conversion.

---

### **4. The 18th Century: The Collapse of Central Power**

As the Mughal Empire weakened after the death of Aurangzeb (1707), power shifted to regional entities like the **Marathas**, **Sikhs**, and **Nawabs**.

* **The Peshwa Rule (Maratha Empire):** In Western India, under the Brahmin Peshwas, caste laws became **extremely draconian**. This is often cited as the "darkest period" for Avarnas (Mahars and Mangs).
* They were reportedly forced to carry a **broom** to sweep away their footprints and a **pot** around their necks to catch their spit, so they wouldn't pollute the ground or the air for the "upper" castes.


* **The Battle of Bhima Koregaon (1818):** This period of intense oppression led many Mahars (Avarnas) to join the British East India Company's army to fight against the Peshwa, seeing the British as a potential "liberator" from caste tyranny.

### **The Prelude to British Rule**

When the British began taking over in the mid-1700s, they didn't find a "chaotic" society—they found a **hyper-organized** one. The British "Census" projects of the 1800s didn't create caste, but they **recorded and rigidified** these 2,000-year-old oral traditions into official British law, which changed the game forever.

**Would you like me to explain how the British Census of 1871 "mapped" these castes and actually made the hierarchy even more rigid?**

The British arrival marked a transition from a **customary** caste system to a **codified** one. While pre-colonial caste was brutal, it was often localized and varied. The British, obsessed with "scientific" classification, turned it into a rigid legal framework.

### **The Great Categorization: The Census of 1871**

The British wanted to understand the millions of people they now ruled. Led by officials like **Herbert Hope Risley**, they used the 1871 (and later 1901) Census to "map" every single Indian into a specific slot.

* **Anthropometry:** Risley believed in "race science." He used calipers to measure the noses and skulls of Indians, theorizing that "nasal index" correlated with caste—the narrower the nose, the higher the caste.
* **The Ranking War:** For the first time, the government officially ranked castes in order of "social precedence." This caused a frenzy; thousands of petitions were filed by various groups claiming they were higher than the Census had placed them.
* **Fixing the Fluidity:** Before this, a group might claim a higher status in one century and move up. Once it was written in the British "Blue Books," your caste was legally frozen.

---

### **Caste Becomes a Legal Identity**

By documenting caste in every official transaction, the British inadvertently made it the primary way Indians interacted with the state.

1. **The Courts:** The British decided to apply "Hindu Law" to Hindus. They relied on Brahmin scholars (*Pandits*) to interpret ancient texts like the *Manusmriti*. This effectively turned a 2,000-year-old religious text into the **civil law of the land**, giving scriptural oppression the backing of British police and courts.
2. **Land Ownership:** The **1900 Punjab Land Alienation Act** and similar laws restricted land ownership to certain "agricultural castes." This effectively barred many Avarna groups from ever owning land, legally enforcing their status as landless laborers.
3. **Criminal Tribes Act (1871):** In one of the most oppressive moves, the British labeled entire castes (mostly nomadic or forest-dwelling Avarnas) as "hereditary criminals." Being born into these tribes meant you were a criminal by birth, requiring you to register with the police and limiting your movement.

---

### **The Rise of the "Depressed Classes" (1900–1947)**

As the independence movement grew, the question of the Avarna (now called the **"Depressed Classes"** by the British) became a central political battle.

* **The Divide:** The British began using the "minority" status of Dalits to counter the Hindu-dominated Congress party’s demand for independence.
* **The Ambedkar-Gandhi Conflict:** This culminated in the **Poona Pact of 1932**.
* **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** (himself a Mahar/Avarna) demanded separate electorates (where only Dalits vote for Dalit leaders).
* **Mahatma Gandhi** fasted unto death against this, fearing it would permanently split Hindu society.
* **The Result:** The "Reserved Seats" system we see in India today was born—a compromise where seats are held for Dalits, but the entire population votes.

The word **Dalit** is a Sanskrit/Marathi word that means **"broken," "crushed," or "ground down."** Unlike the term "Avarna," which is a religious classification, or "Harijan," which was a paternalistic term coined by Gandhi, "Dalit" is a term of **political identity and pride**.

### **When was the term coined?**

While the root word exists in ancient Sanskrit, its application as a specific social and political identity is relatively modern:

* **Late 19th Century (Roots):** The term was first used in a socio-political context by **Jyotirao Phule**, a radical social reformer in Maharashtra, in the 1880s. He used it to describe the "broken" condition of the outcasts who were oppressed by the Brahminical social order.
* **1920s–30s (Ambedkar’s Era):** Dr. B.R. Ambedkar occasionally used "Dalit" in his Marathi speeches and writings to describe the Depressed Classes, though the official legal term at the time remained "Scheduled Castes."
* **1970s (The Turning Point):** The term gained global prominence and widespread usage with the rise of the **Dalit Panthers** in 1972. Inspired by the Black Panther Party in the U.S., these activists and poets reclaimed the word "Dalit" as a symbol of militant defiance, rejecting the "submissive" connotations of older terms.

### **How far back can we trace this classification?**

While the *word* is modern, the *classification* of a group of people existing "outside" the social order—the people we now call Dalits—can be traced back through several layers of history:

#### **1. The "Avarna" (c. 1000 BCE – 500 BCE)**

The most ancient classification is **Avarna** (those without *Varna*).

* As the Indo-Aryan social structure solidified, indigenous tribes who were conquered or refused to assimilate were placed outside the four-fold system.
* They were often referred to as **Dasas** or **Dasyus** in the earliest Vedas, though these terms were more ethnic/tribal than purely caste-based at first.

#### **2. The "Chandala" and "Panchama" (c. 200 BCE – 200 CE)**

The classification became a "fifth group" (**Panchama**) as society became more agrarian.

* The **Manusmriti** officially codified the **Chandala** as the lowest of the low.
* By this point, the classification wasn't just "outside the system"; it was "systemically excluded." They were the "Antyaja" (those born at the very end/lowest).

#### **3. The "Untouchables" (c. 400 CE – 1100 CE)**

As we discussed earlier, the travelogues of Chinese monks like **Faxian** (400 CE) and **Xuanzang** (600 CE) provide the first external proof that this classification had become a physical reality. This is when the classification turned into a "walking biohazard" in the eyes of the elite—the "Untouchable."

#### **4. The "Depressed Classes" (1800s – 1935)**

When the British arrived, they struggled with these terms. They initially used "Pariahs" (a term from a specific Tamil caste) or "Outcasts."

* By the early 20th century, the British administration settled on the **"Depressed Classes."**
* The **Government of India Act of 1935** finally replaced this with **"Scheduled Castes" (SC)**, a legal term still used in the Indian Constitution today for administrative purposes.

### **1. Historical Records of Conversion**

For over 2,500 years, lower-caste and Avarna (Dalit) communities have used conversion as a tool for social and spiritual emancipation.

* **Ancient (Buddhism/Jainism):** The earliest records (c. 6th century BCE) show individuals from "polluted" occupations joining Buddhist sanghas to escape the Varna hierarchy.
* **Medieval (Islam/Sikhism):** Travelers like Ibn Battuta and Sufi records document entire occupational guilds (Jatis) converting to Islam for social dignity. Later, the Sikh movement explicitly rejected the "sacred thread" of caste.
* **Colonial (Christianity):** 19th-century missionary and Census records show "Mass Movements" where tens of thousands of Dalits converted to Christianity to access education and legal protections.
* **Modern (Navayana Buddhism):** The most significant documented event was in 1956, when Dr. B.R. Ambedkar led 500,000 Dalits to embrace Buddhism in Nagpur as a final "exit" from Hinduism.

### **2. The Legal Response: Anti-Conversion Laws**

As these exits became a threat to the traditional social order and political demographics, various "Freedom of Religion" laws were enacted to regulate or prevent them.

* **Origins:** First created by Hindu-led Princely States in the 1930s to block Christian missionaries, these laws have now expanded to 12 Indian states as of 2026.

* **The Notification Wall:** Many states require Dalits to notify the government months before converting. This often exposes them to social boycotts or violence from dominant-caste groups before the conversion can even take place.

* **Loss of Rights:** Under current law, a Dalit who converts to **Islam or Christianity** legally loses their "Scheduled Caste" status. This means they lose access to government job quotas, university seats, and political representation.

how does the RSS see caste problems?

The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) has a complex and evolving relationship with the caste system. As a Hindu nationalist organization, its primary goal is **Sangathan** (consolidation)—uniting all Hindus into a single, powerful social and political block. Because the caste system creates internal friction and divisions, the RSS views it as a major obstacle to this goal.

However, their approach to "solving" the problem differs significantly from the radical, structural critiques of leaders like Dr. B.R. Ambedkar.

---

### **1. The Core Philosophy: "Samarasta" (Harmony)**

The RSS does not typically use the language of "Social Justice" (which focuses on rights and power-sharing); instead, they use the term **Samajik Samarasta** (Social Harmony).

* **The Vision:** They believe that all Hindus, regardless of caste, are part of the same "national body." They view the four *Varnas* as having once been a harmonious division of labor that later became "corrupted" by external invasions and internal decay.
* **The Goal:** Rather than abolishing the *concept* of caste identity entirely, they seek to eliminate the **stigma** and **hierarchy** associated with it. They want a Brahmin and a Dalit to feel like "brothers" while still maintaining their Hindu identity.

---

### **2. Practical Efforts: One Well, One Temple, One Crematorium**

The RSS’s most concrete campaign against untouchability is the **"Ek Kuan, Ek Mandir, Ek Smashan"** (One Well, One Temple, One Crematorium) initiative.

* **Objective:** To ensure that in every village, all Hindus use the same water source, pray in the same temple, and use the same cremation grounds.
* **Inclusion:** The RSS has actively encouraged the training of Dalit priests and the inclusion of Dalits in the *Shakha* (their basic unit of training). In a *Shakha*, everyone wears the same uniform and eats together, which is a direct (though silent) challenge to traditional "commensality" (eating) taboos.

---

### **3. The Internal Tension: Reform vs. Tradition**

The RSS faces a balancing act between its reformist goals and its traditionalist base.

* **The "Siege" Perspective:** The RSS often argues that caste became rigid and oppressive primarily due to "foreign invasions" (Islamic and British) which forced Hindu society to turn inward and become defensive. Historians generally view this as a selective interpretation, as records of caste oppression (like the *Manusmriti*) predate these invasions by centuries.
* **Resistance to Reservations:** Historically, the RSS was wary of the **Reservation system (Affirmative Action)**, fearing it would institutionalize caste identity forever and prevent "national integration." However, in recent decades, they have publicly stated their firm support for reservations as long as social inequality exists.

---

### **4. RSS vs. Ambedkar: Different Paths to the Same End?**

The RSS has made a massive effort to "claim" Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as a national hero, but their ideologies remain distinct:

| Feature | Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's View | RSS View |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Root of Problem** | Hindu Scriptures (Manusmriti, etc.). | "Corruptions" that crept in over time. |
| **Solution** | **Annihilation of Caste** (total destruction of the system). | **Social Harmony** (keeping the community together but ending discrimination). |
| **Method** | Political rights, legal safeguards, and conversion out of Hinduism. | Social integration, shared rituals, and "Ghar Wapsi" (reconversion). |

This has allowed them to build a broad Hindu electoral coalition that bridges traditional caste gaps.

**Subaltern Hindutva** is a term used by political scientists (notably Badri Narayan) to describe the strategy of the RSS and BJP to mobilize lower-caste groups—specifically **Non-Jatav Dalits** and **Most Backward Castes (MBCs)**—who felt excluded by the dominant regional parties.

The core of this strategy is to give these groups a sense of "Hindu pride" by linking their local caste histories to the broader struggle for the "protection of the motherland."

---

### **1. Uttar Pradesh: The Laboratory (2014–Present)**

Since 2014, Uttar Pradesh has been the most prominent example of Subaltern Hindutva. The goal was to break the hold of the Samajwadi Party (Yadav-focused) and the Bahujan Samaj Party (Jatav-focused).

* **The Strategy:** The RSS identified smaller, marginalized castes and reimagined their ancestors as "Defenders of the Faith."
* **Caste Groups Involved:**
* **Pasis:** Re-branded as descendants of *Suheldev*, a legendary king said to have defeated the nephew of Mahmud of Ghazni.
* **Kushwahas, Shakyas, and Mauryas:** Linked to the glory of the Maurya Empire and reimagined as defenders of Dharma.
* **Lodh-Rajputs:** Mobilized early on (starting in the 1990s with Kalyan Singh) as traditional agrarian defenders.


* **The Result:** In the **2017 and 2022 Assembly elections**, a massive percentage of non-Jatav Dalits and non-Yadav OBCs voted for the BJP, shifting the power dynamic of the state.

---

### **2. Bihar: Breaking the Mandal Monopoly (2015–Present)**

In Bihar, the RSS/BJP sought to penetrate the "Mandal" politics of Lalu Prasad Yadav and Nitish Kumar by appealing to the **Extremely Backward Castes (EBCs)**.

* **The Strategy:** Instead of focusing on the dominant Yadavs or Kurmis, they focused on the "micro-castes" that felt these dominant groups took all the benefits of reservations.
* **Caste Groups Involved:**
* **Mallahs (Fishermen):** Re-identified through the mythology of the Nishad community.
* **Musahars:** A highly marginalized Dalit group. The RSS works heavily in their colonies through *Ekal Vidyalayas* (one-teacher schools).


* **Key Year:** **2020 Assembly Elections**, where the EBC vote bank became a decisive factor in maintaining the NDA government’s power.

---

### **3. West Bengal: The New Frontier (2019–Present)**

Subaltern Hindutva was the primary engine for the BJP’s rise in West Bengal, where caste was historically suppressed by the "class-based" rhetoric of the Left Front.

* **The Strategy:** Highlighting the "persecution" of Dalit refugees from Bangladesh and contrasting their status with the state’s treatment of religious minorities.
* **Caste Groups Involved:**
* **Matuas (Namasudras):** A Dalit refugee sect. The BJP’s promise of the **Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA)** was specifically tailored to give this group citizenship and dignity.
* **Rajbanshis:** A semi-tribal/Dalit group in North Bengal who felt ignored by the Kolkata-based "Bhadralok" (upper-caste) elite.


* **Key Year:** **2019 General Elections**, where the BJP won 18 seats in Bengal, largely carried by the Matua and Rajbanshi vote.
